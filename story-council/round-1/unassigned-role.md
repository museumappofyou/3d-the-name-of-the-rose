# Story council — unassigned seat

**Date:** 2 October 2026
**Seat:** Unassigned. The brief’s role line was left as a placeholder, and no assignment list followed. This report therefore answers the whole brief. Its bias is the question the project has not yet proved: what the player repeatedly does, for more than an hour, inside this particular abbey.
**Spoiler policy:** Architectural and institutional facts only. No deaths, culprits, solutions, hidden motives, or late outcomes from *The Name of the Rose*. Novel-derived play is mentioned only as a labelled, optional mode. The existing altar proof is treated as a novel-derived route that must stay behind that label. Its mechanism is not described.

Labels used below: **NOVEL-SUPPORTED** (the reconstruction’s own evidence), **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** (external practice that can fit this house), **ORIGINAL FICTION** (authored for the game).

## What this abbey can already tell

The native game is still a slice: porch, cloister, church, and the chapel stair. The browser reference already walks the whole enclosure: Aedificium (kitchen and refectory, scriptorium, library), church, cloister range, hospice, infirmary and gardens, cemetery, farmyard, smithy, and gate. Thirty people exist as bodies and jobs. Five are fitted in the native proof. One, Alinardo, has a real schedule. Those places are **NOVEL-SUPPORTED** as architecture.

What is already a game system, rather than a picture:

- A late-November day divided into eight offices and twenty-one phases, from night watch through Matins, Lauds, Prime, work, Terce, Sext, the midday meal, Nones, Vespers, supper, a short procession, Compline, and retirement. Daylight is a narrow winter window. A curfew rule can bar a door from the end of supper until Lauds and still let someone inside leave.
- Doors and pivots share one state with collision and with sound. Opening a way is not the same as having walked it. A study jump, teleport, or load cannot invent a traversal.
- The choir’s sound is already a spatial fact. Openings and a leak path change where chant seems to come from, how loud it is, and how muffled it is.
- Knowledge is a short list of earned notes, an optional speaker, the hour, rooms actually entered, and a route index. Unknown note ids are rejected. The notebook is a folio, not a quest log.
- Saves keep clock, portal state, place, and knowledge, and recover from a damaged file.
- Conditions are a closed vocabulary: known facts, phase, office, hour window, portal state, curfew. There is no scripting language in the data.

Not yet a game: testimony that can be false, custody, a conclusion the player submits, or any original case. The crowd of forty-six is a drawing test. A discovery is still stored as true once seen.

The systems an ordinary adventure game does not have are the ones to build on. Acoustic transport can make a sentence possible or impossible from a given walk. Passage tracking separates “I was told there is a stair” from “I went down it.” The horarium can empty a room because everyone is in choir. A curfew that still lets you leave can make a threshold into evidence. Darkness, chant, and the difference between work and a meal are already data.

**What can this abbey tell that a conversation in a room could not?** Stories whose truth depends on a body, an hour, an earshot, and which side of a door you are on.

## Evaluating the stated identity

“Historical environmental investigation through knowledge, access, architecture and time” is the right family and the wrong emphasis if all four nouns are treated as equal.

Knowledge, access, architecture, and time are the instruments. The activity is judging a human situation that those instruments make decidable. If the instruments become the subject, the game is a well-made museum with errands. Players will forgive a great deal of stone if someone they can name is about to be blamed, deprived of a book, fed against a fast, or remembered under the wrong stone. They will not forgive a correct account of catchwords.

The identity also over-claims history. The clock is a reconstruction convention for one late-November day, not a finding about every house in 1327. The player needs to trust the bell, not audit the spreadsheet. The Rule’s seasonal pattern is real; the decimals in the data are fiction. That distinction is enough.

The identity is right to refuse combat, experience, crafting, loot, markers, fetch quests, detective vision, endless trees, and switch puzzles. Those refusals should stay. They are not yet a promise of many hours. Many hours require a reason to return to a corridor you already understand, because the corridor means something else after Vespers, or because yesterday’s conclusion changed who will speak to you.

NPCs do not make this an RPG. They make testimony possible. Three people with duties, possessions, and something to lose are enough. Forty-six people with daily needs would be a different product.

## Three structures

### 1. Commissions in one winter

You are a literate guest with a narrow brief: collate a loan, settle a quarrel of records, say what can actually be known. You are not master of the house, and you are not a visiting genius from the novel.

A session starts at a gate, a hospice stair, or a reading stand, with one question and one person who will be hurt by a bad or late answer. You walk, listen, read two short texts, stand where a witness stood, wait until an office moves people, and write an account. Knowledge sticks inside the case and, lightly, between cases: which door shuts, who carries books, how long the cloister takes. It does not become a skill tree.

Time is one or two days of the existing horarium. A missed window leaves another record tomorrow. It does not fail you. People know their work and answer better when you bring a contradiction you have already seen. Routes, sightlines, and thickness are the test rig.

The ending is a submitted account that separates what is established from what is only blamed. The house then acts on the established part: a book returns, a penance is withheld, a stone is left alone. Replay is the next commission, plus a second path through the same evidence. The past does not reshuffle.

The novel supplies the building, the season, the offices, and the fear of a misread book. Novel episodes are a separate, labelled door. This structure fits the slice, the save model, and a small team. Its weakness is that episodes can feel staged unless a few consequences survive them.

### 2. One office, one season

You hold a temporary obedience for a winter — guest-master’s clerk, or the armarius’s assistant — and the stories are what the office dumps on the table. A session starts with the duty already moving: a cart at the gate, a quire that must be ruled before Terce, a patron’s servant who will not sit.

The repeated act is a small clerical job. Investigation is the moment the job does not match the house. Knowledge is institutional memory: by season’s end you know the cellarer’s habits better than any single puzzle. Time is the liturgical season — fasts, slaughter around Martinmas, a promised visitation, the shortening day — not a clue timer. People remember whether you exposed them, protected them, or repeated their words. That memory is the persistence. No friendship meter.

The plan stays stable. Use of the plan changes: a boarded opening, a moved desk, a lamp in a different walk. The ending is the office handed back, with a quire of decisions, some still uncertain. This is the structure that can last many hours without cloning a formula. It is also one step from the custodian simulator the project should refuse, and it needs more world than the native slice has.

### 3. A readable house, and no assigned crime

You are here to learn how the house holds together. Curiosity is the brief. A session starts wherever you left off, at whatever bell is striking. You follow a sound, a light, or a margin, come back after dark, and build a private account of what is older, what was repaired, and what the catalogue pretends. Some threads never close.

People are neighbours on a schedule. Talk is optional. Nobody is required to advance a quest, so nobody is forced to explain the plot. The ending, if you want one, is your own unsigned description of the house. The game does not grade it.

This uses the asset that already exists and is the safest spoiler posture, provided secret routes stay opt-in. It may also be a beautiful afternoon rather than a game. Without a moment of judgment, “understand the abbey” runs out when the stone becomes familiar.

### Which one

Favor commissions in one winter, hung on a thin clerical duty so that walking between cases has a reason. Each unit still ends in a submitted account. A repair, a corrected line, or a person who will not meet your eye can persist. That is the whole continuity. It is not management and not a generator.

The season-long office is a later product, if the first commissions are loved. The ungraded visit is the right public web viewer. Novel-derived episodes sit beside the commissions, named and opt-in, one situation at a time.

## Ten original commissions

Selection sketches, not scripts. Named people are **ORIGINAL FICTION**. They are not the novel’s brothers in a thin disguise. The leaf and the double receipt refine drafts already in the design notes. The other eight are new.

### 1. The Leaf Before Vespers

A gathering’s last leaf is missing from a manuscript about to leave the house. A novice seen with a wrapped book is already being blamed. The copyist moved the leaf into a choir book to flatten a damp edge, and was vague about when he last had it. The novice’s parcel is a different repair. Treat the damp-press as this man’s improvisation unless a sourced exemplar is chosen first.

The player wants to know what that sighting can prove, and which leaf belongs to the gathering. Cloister, church, and a reading stand are already in the native slice. Evidence is the loan slip, a catchword, the parcel’s size, one sightline, and two wordings from the copyist. Systems: spoken claims, one hour window, physical passage, a conclusion that rejects “I found a page.” Vespers is the witness’s hinge. Miss the live crossing and a slate the next morning still works. Copyist, novice, and witness want an undamaged-looking book, a clear name, and not to be called a liar. Finding the leaf settles identity of the object only. **SMALL.** Highest leverage in the project, and the easiest case to solve without walking if the slips do all the work. The novice’s punishment has to be close and public or the stake is mild.

### 2. The Name on the Stone

A patron’s anniversary mass is prepared for a father the sacrist places under the newer stone by the church’s north wall. The necrology and a worn stone disagree about the name and the year. Nobody has died this week. The family is already in the hospice, and the house is about to pray for the wrong man.

The player wants to know whom the house thinks it is remembering, and which record it quietly corrected. The cemetery between church, Aedificium, and garden, and the evening file to the north door, are **NOVEL-SUPPORTED** places. The dispute is fiction. Evidence: two stones, a feast-dated letter, a mason’s mark shared with a repair, who pauses in the procession. Hours matter once: after supper the sacrist will not stop, and the file shows who still bows to the worn stone. “By the north wall” picks a different grave from the guest door than from the north door. Sacrist, mason’s son, and a guest remembering a childhood funeral may all be sincere. The player separates a liturgical date from a copied year and may still not know who ordered the new stone. **MEDIUM.** The mystery is misremembering, not a culprit. Risk: inscription homework. If a tooltip explains both stones, the walk was wasted. The cemetery is not in the native slice.

### 3. The Voice in the Wrong Walk

During Sext a brother claims he heard a quarrel in the choir that named him, and he wants a public correction. Others say the choir was only singing. He may have heard a different opening, at a different moment, and supplied the insult himself.

The player wants to know what that man could have heard from where he stood. Choir sound already changes level and muffling through specific openings. Evidence: the claimed sentence, the office that was actually sung, door state, two standing positions, someone in the nave who heard only the psalm. Sext is short and essential. The same sentence during work, with the choir empty, carries differently. The player learns that by waiting, not by reading a decibel. The offended man, the cantor, and a novice who was late are enough. Pride is the motive. Separate “he is lying” from “he heard something and made it about himself.” **SMALL to MEDIUM.** This is the case the audio code was accidentally built for. Risk: players who cannot hear the difference are locked out unless a transcript is a real path, and the answer must not collapse into “the door was shut.”

### 4. The Bell and the Oven

A novice is accused of missing Prime. He says he was in choir before the bell finished. The baker says the office bell and his oven-signal came in the wrong order, and that the boy was still in the yard. A petty, real punishment is about to be imposed. The Rule’s two signals before an office are **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED**. This morning is fiction.

The player wants to know which signal the house obeyed. Kitchen, yard, and church are far enough apart that “I heard the bell” is a place. Evidence: an empty or filled choir stall, ash in the oven, a note that Prime started late, snow on a hem. Two honest people can be on different clocks. The player can walk the lower bound from oven to choir. The baker is defending his own punctuality as much as accusing the boy. **SMALL.** Timekeeping is the suspect. Risk: a seconds-level replay. Keep the windows wide. Needs the kitchen approach, outside the current cells.

### 5. The Loaf on a Fast

Guests were given a festal portion on a day the sacrist’s calendar calls a fast and the cellarer’s tablet calls the patron’s anniversary. Both entries are in last year’s hand. The guests are still at table. Cellarers feeding refectory, infirmary, and guests are **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** in well-documented houses. The sliding date is fiction.

The player wants to know which calendar the house is living, and who gains if the patron’s day covers a fast. Before the meal there is paper. During it the evidence is edible and the guests will talk. After it the story hardens. A feast name is not a civil date. Fraud, a local privilege, and a copyist’s slip are not the same sin. **MEDIUM.** Keep the documents to two pages and the bread visible. Needs refectory and cellar.

### 6. The Window and the Tally

The glazier’s account says three church lights were releaded. The church shows two new lights and one old. Matching glass sits in an unfinished reliquary, and again in a hospice window the sacrist did not authorize. Smithy, church, and hospice are **NOVEL-SUPPORTED** places. The diversion is fiction.

The player wants to know where the church’s glass went and who had the right to spend it. Daylight is for judging glass. After dark the hospice lamp makes the colour obvious from the garden. One night look, not a schedule puzzle. Glazier, sacrist, and guest-master. Prove the movement of the glass without having to prove the bargain. **MEDIUM.** Eyes do the collation. Do not migrate three buildings just to host it, and do not make the matching panel glow.

### 7. The Line the Catalogue Lost

A scriptorium shelf list omits a title a donor says he gave. The book is on a desk, rebound, with a new first leaf and an erased title on the board. A living patron is about to be told his gift was never here. Medieval titles are unstable, and opening words identify a text better than a modern title. **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED.** The incident is fiction. Keep it on the scriptorium floor. Do not send it into the restricted upper library.

The player wants to know whether the house is hiding a book, renaming it, or whether the donor misremembered. Copyists are at the desks between offices and gone when the bell fills the choir. Either absence or presence should be a workable path. Armarius, the copyist who rebound it, the donor’s clerk. Opening words settle what the object is. Why it was retitled can stay partly opaque. **MEDIUM.** Translate everything. Two books, not a catalogue minigame. Needs the scriptorium.

### 8. The Herb Named by Its Stems

Under snow the garden shows stalks. The infirmarian sent the kitchen a bundle for a guest’s cough. The kitchen cooked part of it as a familiar pot-herb. The bundle still in the infirmary is a different plant. The guest is no better. No one has been poisoned. Someone has been treated under the wrong name. Separate infirmary beds and the winter difficulty of naming dried plants are **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** in outline. The mix-up is fiction.

The player wants to know which plant was intended, which was cooked, and who is trusting a winter name. The cut was after Nones. A path in snow shows which bed was taken. Infirmarian, cook, guest. The cook has used that stalk for ten winters and is not a fool. Names have drifted between garden, book, and pot. **SMALL to MEDIUM.** The distinction must be visible — cut bed, string, the taste the guest describes — not a Latin herbal exam. Needs garden and infirmary.

### 9. The Second Receipt

A tenant’s grain was received once. Two receipts say so, on different days, for a holding under two names. The cellarer is ready to call the tenant a forger. The older name is what the hill was called before a boundary stone moved. The house’s copyist doubled the obligation by updating one name only. Obedientiary accounts and renamed holdings are **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** as a pattern, best documented in English Benedictine archives and only analogous here. The incident is the design notes’ rent case, kept because the twist is administrative.

The player wants to know which obligation was discharged, and which name is the land. Carts come in work hours. The porter will not leave the gate during an office. Forgery is the lazy reading. Cellarer, porter, tenant’s son. The son may be right and inarticulate. **MEDIUM.** The tenant’s fear has to be in a face in the first five minutes. Needs gate and granary, not the library.

### 10. The Older Door

A building account pays for stone “at the old door of the apostles.” The chapter house stands on an earlier church, and its surviving portal already shows the apostles. The sacrist says that portal is the old door. A floor scar says a second opening was blocked when the new pointed door was cut. The blocked opening hid no treasure. It hid a custom: processions still bow to a threshold that now sits inside a cupboard. The older church and the two doors are **NOVEL-SUPPORTED**. The payment and the cupboard are fiction. This is “The Wall That Remembers” without a scandal that needs a secret plot.

The player wants to know which opening the house means, and what custom is still attached to it. After supper the procession uses one meaning. At Prime the chapter uses the new door. Same words, two ceremonies. Sacrist, an older monk who bows at an empty-looking place, a mason. **MEDIUM.** Architecture is the contested text. If the reward is a hidden room, the case has betrayed itself. The chapter house is not in the native slice. Do not invent a level underground.

## Deep dives

### The Leaf Before Vespers

**Opening.** The player is here to collate a manuscript before it leaves. A novice is stopped in the cloister with a wrapped parcel and told he will answer in chapter. The gathering shows the stub of the missing leaf. The public blame is why anyone cares.

**Topology.** Three knots, any order. The object: slip, stub, catchword, the extra leaf in the choir book, the parcel. The sentence: where the witness stood, then the copyist’s vague answer, then a sharper one only after the choir book has been seen. The person: what the novice was actually sent to carry. Ask too early and you get the weather. Find the book first and the second sentence arrives sooner. The afternoon itself does not change.

**Evidence and contradictions.** Stub, damp edge, and parcel size are seen. Catchword and loan description are read. The three speakers stay quotations. Both the witness and the novice truly involve a wrapped book, which does not identify the leaf. The copyist’s time-word fits his walk and not the witness’s view. Dampness fits a press, not a theft.

**Architecture and time.** One range, one church door, one stand. Walking the claim should make the carrier appear and vanish. If the slips settle it alone, cut slips. Next Vespers shows the real errand, because repairs are still carried. Miss it and a morning slate carries the same fact. No frame-perfect catch.

**People.** The novice knows the parcel and describes it badly. The copyist knows the leaf. The witness knows a silhouette and has already made it a story. An optional leak over one desk explains the dampness and is not required. A prior false blame changes how hard the player leans, not which page it is.

**Deduction, uncertainty, ending.** One account, three parts: which object, who moved it, what the sighting is worth. Collation may confirm the object. Location must not auto-name the mover. Returning the right leaf while accusing the novice fails with that gap named, and no hint of a room. Calling the copyist malicious can stand. Motive may stay open. Custody should not. The leaf goes back. The player speaks structured phrases in front of the person ready to punish. Any penalty is a recommendation the house may ignore. No suspect menu. Going to the choir first changes only the copyist’s opening sentence.

### The Name on the Stone

**Opening.** Evening in the hospice. A family has been shown a grave. The player is asked to read the name so the mass will be right. The stone’s year does not match the letter. The sacrist is already copying the formulary.

**Topology.** Stones and the walk between them. A necrology and a letter that date the death by a feast. A procession that shows who still bows to the worn stone. A bill for when the new stone was cut. The bow is missable. The books are not. Missing the bow makes a colder case that can still be finished.

**Evidence and contradictions.** The new stone is recent and confident. The necrology is older and uses a feast. A kinship word in the letter could mean father or uncle. The sacrist treats a clean cutting as proof. “By the wall” is the north door’s grave after supper and a different grave from the guests’ west door.

**Time and people.** After supper the sacrist will not break rank. After Compline a lamp makes the worn letters readable. At Prime the story gets smoother because the mass is close. The sacrist knows the donor’s preference. The mason’s son knows the order, not the body. The guest remembers a season and a bell. The old monk knows where he was taught to bow. A third broken marker in the grass, neither man, is optional and changes the scale of the family’s certainty.

**Deduction, uncertainty, ending.** Say whom the mass can honestly name, and which record was altered to flatter the living. Naming the person who paid for the recut should be marked unsupported. Choosing the new stone because the family is here is an acceptable, comfortable account. Do not stamp it wrong. A later commission can show the old monk’s silence. Before the mass the player returns a name, a date basis, and what is still disputed. The sacrist may override them. Hearing which name is sung is the ending. Skipping the procession on purpose is a thinner true route. Do not force the bow into a cutscene.

### The Voice in the Wrong Walk

**Opening.** A brother stops the player before chapter and repeats an insult he says came out of the choir at Sext. Each retelling has made the wording sharper. Chapter is after Prime tomorrow.

**Topology.** Stand where he stood. Hear the next Sext, or one demonstration by the cantor with the doors as the player sets them. Learn what was actually sung. Notice a second opening: a nave errand, muffled, sounding a little like a name. Door state is a test, not an item. After the test he may retreat from “my name” to “a voice.” That retreat is evidence. A laugh the novice heard behind him, not in the choir, is a trap for anyone hunting a villain. It can be irrelevant.

**Evidence and architecture.** Sound, with a transcript. The appointed office. Two listener positions. Testimony that changes under a test. Use ordinary church doors. If the slice’s special leak belongs to a novel secret, leave it unused. A case that needs a spoiler mechanism is the wrong case. The same walk during work teaches what an office sounds like, as against Sext.

**People.** He knows his fear and his increasingly exact story. The cantor knows the music and dislikes libel against the office. The novice knows where he stood. The person whose voice may have carried was discussing an errand and connects it only if asked about the errand. One corner where the choir becomes suddenly plain should remain useful in later cases. That corner is the lasting find.

**Deduction, uncertainty, ending.** What was hearable, what the listener added, whether a name was spoken. A valid account can say that no insult was spoken, that a muffled phrase could be misheard, and that the man still deserves a kind answer. The last of those is a judgment, not a checked fact. Confirming the insult can send chapter after someone on an errand. Allow it, and later show that person’s confusion. Do not produce a hidden villain to repair the moral. The account is read with place and door-state attached. The player does not assign penance. The Rule’s scale — private warning, then public rebuke — belongs to the house. Testing the doors in another order changes how hard he commits. The acoustic fact stays put.

## A reusable framework

A commission needs a header and a few records. It does not need a narrative programming language.

Build once, and reuse: stable people with a duty, a possession, a route, and availability, where the mesh never invents a person; locations, portals, and travel lower bounds; the existing condition vocabulary, extended only when a case demands a new operator; observations that record what was perceived, where, when, by which sense, and what that does not establish; claims tied to a speaker or a document, which never write themselves into the fact list; custody of objects over time; a submitted account made of links the player chooses, checked against authored constraints; a save of all of that, with a scenario id and version.

Leave as content, not systems: the prose, which contradictions are live, which partial accounts are defensible, what the house does afterward, and the provenance class of optional discoveries.

Keep out of the first framework a general hypothesis engine, a dialogue-graph tool, random variants, reputation numbers, and “any person can say any line.” Author the lines. Let conditions choose among lines already written.

Implement only the rows the leaf uses. Add acoustic observations for the voice case, then calendrical comparison for the stone. Three cases, three new rows. Provenance stays on the authoring side. A short basis panel may say novel evidence, reconstruction, or fiction. Players should not see claim ids.

## The notebook

The current notebook remembers that something was noticed, once, by whom, and at what hour. That is a good spine and a bad mind. It cannot say “this is only what Martin claims,” cannot be wrong, and cannot change its mind. The moment every note is a true fact with a checkmark, it has become a quest log.

Add only this. Speech notes look like quotations. Inspection notes look like “I saw.” The player may pin two notes and mark the pin “these clash” or “these fit.” A handful of pins, not a board. The player may mark their own doubt. Doubt changes what they see first, not the world. Hours already sort into the day’s ribbon. The map gets a few marks of the kind “I stood here,” and no drawing tools. The account is a separate short page, built when the player is ready, from the pins plus a few structured phrases.

If two notes disagree with a walk the player has done, the game may say that the walk does not fit the time they were told. It may not say who lied. Reading can pause the clock. Waiting on a bench should not. Autosave the latest pins, including the bad theory. Changing your mind is the mechanic. If the notebook takes more than a minute, it is too big.

## Canonical hours

Use the hours as social physics.

Who is in choir, at a desk, in the kitchen, at the gate. A phase change should send a body along a route. It must not teleport the person or swap the mesh. Some men will not talk in procession. Some will talk only at a meal. Alinardo’s availability is already the right pattern. Darkness in a short winter day makes a lamp mean something after Compline that it does not mean at Nones. Chant masks and beacons. A man in choir did not see the yard. That last use is the most honest one.

It becomes tedious when missing an office reduces a meter or ends the day. *La Abadía del Crimen* already showed that obedience-as-timer makes the player resent the bell. It is also tedious to sit through a full office for one sentence, to make every case “where were you at Vespers,” to wait on an empty bench, or to stake a clue on a two-second animation. The right waiting tool is a bench: “until the next bell,” routes play in a compressed way, and the player sees only the path they chose to watch. Menus pause the simulation. The first cases have no irreversible timer. Do not present the fixed decimals as medieval fact. Let the bell be the authority, and keep the reconstruction note in the basis panel.

## Secrets that are not objectives

A secret is worth finding when the house becomes a different house afterward.

Older fabric: the chapter house on a burnt church, a threshold that no longer opens, a change in floor level. Institutional drift: who really keeps the key, which fast is observed, which winter office is sung short. Catalogue ghosts: a donor’s boast, a book rebound under another name, a loan that has lasted twenty years. People in their work: a novice who rules lines better than the copyist, a porter who reads numbers and not Latin. Repairs and the same mason’s mark on a pier and a grave. One corner of the cloister where the choir is suddenly plain. Seasonal work, slaughter and brewing and snow on the beds. Guest traces in the hospice. An inscription the house itself cannot agree on, left unresolved, with no collection counter.

Skip tallies, map fog, and shimmering pickups. If a discovery changes no later sentence, route, or sense of a room, it is clutter. Novel secrets stay behind an explicit opt-in. Spend original invention on ordinary rooms first. The library labyrinth is a poor first mystery and the part of the reconstruction most likely to retell the book.

## Historical authenticity

History constrains plausibility. It is not a quarry.

A winter sequence of night office, Lauds near dawn, Prime, Terce, work, Sext, one meal, Nones, Vespers, Compline, then quiet is **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** by the Rule’s pattern from October to Lent, including a double signal to break off work. The minute boundaries in the data are **ORIGINAL FICTION** for one late-November day. The bell is authoritative inside the fiction.

A handful of obedientiaries — cellarer, sacrist, cantor, infirmarian, guest-master, porter — with lay labour beside them is **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** as a medieval expansion of the Rule. Norwich and Westminster rolls show how far English cathedral priories took this. This fictional mountain house in northern Italy in 1327 is not Norwich. A few offices, not twenty-eight departments.

Private warning, then public rebuke, then separation from table and choir, is **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** as the Rule’s shape for faults. It is not a criminal court. Inquisitorial procedure is real in the fourteenth century and is also the novel’s weather. As a default ending it will pull original cases back into the book.

Catchwords, unstable titles, opening words, and scraped parchment are **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** and already in the project’s notes. The pecia system belongs to university stationers, not to an ordinary monastic scriptorium. Do not import it because it is interesting.

Rentals as selective memory, dates by feast or kalends that sometimes disagree, November slaughter, and an infirmary garden kept off the table are fair material. **HISTORICALLY SUPPORTED** in outline, and the slaughter season is also **NOVEL-SUPPORTED** here. A specific plant’s winter appearance needs a real herbal before anyone calls it typical. Most lines in a real cartulary are dull and true. The interesting line is the one someone “clarified.”

These do not fit just because they existed somewhere: university stationers, a full English accounting bureaucracy, chained libraries as the default picture, perpetual silence with elaborate sign-lists, ordeal, or a torture room. Silence here is local. People talk at the gate, at meals, and in the cloister at known hours. Each case note should record class, source, and whether the incident is fiction.

## Other games, as mechanisms only

**Pentiment** spends the day on some people and not others, puts talk at meals, and lets an accusation stick even when no culprit is certified. Steal social choice under partial knowledge. Refuse a murder every act, a clock that taxes reading, and labour minigames. Where this abbey’s evidence is a leaf or a door, let the player be right. Where it is a memory, let them stay unsure.

**Return of the Obra Dinn** will not treat a guess as knowledge. Steal an account with slots. Leave the watch, the death scenes, and the confirmation chime. Here the honest message is “you have the object, not the hand.”

**Heaven’s Vault** lets a later find reinterpret an earlier reading without a reset. Steal that. Do not add a puzzle alphabet. Translate the Latin. Let uncertainty live in attribution.

**Outer Wilds** puts density where there is something to think, and uses no objective marker. Steal the signal. Do not add a time loop. Tomorrow already repeats. Do not hide a clue in every carved capital.

**The Occupation** and **Unheard** give a sentence a place in time and in a plan. Steal that. Refuse a fail-state for reading slowly, and refuse a tape scrubber. A bench that accelerates time, plus a transcript, is the compromise.

**La Abadía del Crimen** is the cautionary adaptation of this book: obedience points, a dark maze, a race through the plot. Do not revive that meter, that labyrinth, or “attend every office” as a goal.

**Myst and Riven** are the reconstruction’s nearer ancestors: learn a place by walking until the place is the problem. Commissions exist so awe does not have to carry the whole game. **Papers, Please** shows that two documents and one discrepancy are enough. Thought-palace interfaces, as in **Disco Elysium** and some Holmes games, pull play out of the room and often grade it. Decline them. **Pathologic**’s institutional weight is useful in small doses between commissions. Its opacity is not.

No case should be pitchable as “Pentiment in 1327.” Take the mechanism, which is always smaller than the game.

## WHAT WE MAY BE GETTING WRONG

1. **Accuracy is being asked to do drama’s job.** A defensible clock and a sourced catchword will not make anyone stay. They stop the team from lying. The player stays because a person is about to be wronged by a confident house.

2. **The first case is too polite.** The leaf is the right systems test and a weak afternoon if the novice’s danger is abstract. Chapter has to be soon, public, and petty.

3. **Documents will eat the abbey.** They are cheaper than behaviour. If the leaf can be solved at one lectern, the world was a lobby. One conclusion in the first case has to be false unless the player has stood in a specific place.

4. **The horarium will become a gimmick by repetition.** Most hours should only change who is nearby. One hour per case should be the instrument. Eight offices are a gift and a trap.

5. **The abbey is being protected into stillness.** A reconstruction wants to stay canonical. A game wants a moved leaf, a corrected line, a man who will not greet you. Without small persistent changes the house is a backdrop.

6. **“Understanding” is too abstract a reward.** Players need to see the explanation land on someone. An account that changes nothing is a book report.

7. **Reverence is narrowing the fiction.** Avoiding the novel’s plot is right. Avoiding argument, fear of error, carved monsters, hunger, and embarrassment would sand off the reasons this setting is not a generic abbey. Atmosphere is not a spoiler.

8. **Three scheduled people may still be exposition.** A route and a duty are not a mind. Each of the first three needs a want that would exist if the player had never arrived.

9. **A short prototype can be mistaken for a structure.** Twenty-five minutes can prove the framework and say nothing about the tenth hour. Choose the second and third prototypes before polishing the first, or the project will optimize a single clever leaf.

10. **Spoiler fear may push everything into the scriptorium.** Church, yard, cemetery, and gate are safer, and right now more distinctive, than another missing quire. The leaf should be first because the slice is there, not because manuscripts are the brand.

## Things we should not build

- A playable retelling, including “original” cases about a forbidden book, a labyrinth, a visiting inquisitor, or a string of deaths. The building is already that novel’s building.
- An obedience meter, or any fail state for missing prayer.
- Monastery management: stocks, morale, crafting, farming yields.
- A generator that deals culprit, object, and room. Two authored cases will teach more than a solver, and the map is full of accidental impossibilities.
- Detective vision, glowing pages, quest markers, and a mind-palace or clue-combination screen.
- Voiced dialogue for the cast. Text in the manuscript interface is already the house style.
- Copying, cooking, or chanting as skill checks. Watching work is useful. A timing bar is not investigation.
- Ultraviolet reading, magic lenses, or a watch that reconstructs the past. Eye, hour, and walk are the instruments.
- The forty-six-person simulation before three people can carry a case across a save.
- Full library migration as the next art goal. Migrate the rooms the next case stands in. The labyrinth is a spoiler surface and a production trap.
- Inquisition trials, torture, and graphic penance as systems.
- Latin as a difficulty gate. Collectible marginalia and completion percentages.
- A time loop, a second playable genius, and a reputation score. Tomorrow already exists. A number will flatten the memory the season needs.
- Web parity with the native cases. The viewer is for reading the building.
- Random truth. Players compare notes. The abbey should have a stable past.

## Recommendation

### GAME THESIS

This should be a first-person investigation in one persistent Benedictine abbey. The player is a literate guest with a temporary clerical duty and a sequence of finite, original commissions. Each commission asks what can honestly be said about a dispute of custody, memory, craft, or blame. The player walks, listens, reads, and waits through the canonical day. Doors, distances, and voices are part of the evidence. Understanding counts when it changes what the house does to a person. The novel supplies the place, the season, the offices, and the fear of a bad reading. It does not supply the cases. A separate, clearly labelled mode may later let a willing player examine situations from the book. The public web build should remain a readable visit.

### PLAYER PROMISE

> When you play this game, you will learn a working abbey well enough to stand in the right walk at the right bell, hear what a witness could not have heard, and say exactly what the evidence can prove — including, sometimes, that a confident house is about to punish the wrong person or remember the wrong name.

### BEST PRODUCT STRUCTURE

Commissions in one winter, hung on a thin persistent office, with free study between them and novel episodes kept opt-in.

### THREE SCENARIOS TO PROTOTYPE

1. **The Leaf Before Vespers** proves documents, blame, one route, and a conclusion in separate parts, inside cells that already run.
2. **The Voice in the Wrong Walk** proves that acoustics, doors, and an office are gameplay. No ordinary adventure template will use that capability.
3. **The Name on the Stone** proves memory, calendrical language, a missable social observation, and an ending that can be pious and incomplete. It also forces a decision about how large the next architectural slice should be.

If only the leaf is fun, this is a manuscript puzzle in a handsome lobby and should be narrowed on purpose. If the voice case is fun, the abbey itself is the engine. If the stone case is fun even when the player is partly wrong, the game can last a season without becoming a courtroom.

### FIRST SCENARIO

**The Leaf Before Vespers.** It is the only one of the three the native slice can host without a new building. Build it meaner than the current draft: chapter is soon, the sightline must be walked, and finding the page must not complete the case.

### SYSTEMS IT PROVES

- Three persistent actors with duties, possessions, and continuous routes on the existing public footprint.
- Claims kept distinct from observations.
- One hour transition and one missed-window alternate.
- Comparison of two short texts and one physical leaf.
- A submitted account with separate support for object, mover, and sighting.
- A save that keeps the player’s pins, including a wrong theory.
- Bench waiting that advances routes without inventing what the player did not watch.
- One ordinary access rule with a way out, only if a real door in the slice can carry it. Otherwise a supervised reading stand.

### WHAT TO POSTPONE

The voice case until the leaf’s account screen exists, then the voice before any cemetery migration for the stone. Also postpone new buildings, novel-derived episodes, the altar route as onboarding, reputation numbers, procedural variants, voiced speech, Latin gates, a mind-map, management, combat, and the web viewer. Do not start a fourth case. If the first three prototypes are dull, rewrite the reasoning before importing another wing.

The leaf leads only if leverage is allowed to dominate. Build it first for that reason alone. It is the least distinctive of the five and the easiest to sleepwalk into a reading exercise.

Protect the voice case in an argument. It is the clearest thing this simulation can do that a visual-novel abbey cannot. The risks — inaccessible audio, fiddly tuning, a puzzle that collapses into a door — are reasons to prototype it second, with transcripts and wide tolerances, not reasons to retreat into another manuscript.

The stone has the best human stake and the best partly-wrong ending. It loses on leverage because the cemetery is not in the native slice. Migrating a wing in order to feel something is how this project postpones the game again.

The older door and the second receipt are later commissions. One tempts a secret-room payoff. The other tempts a ledger. Keep them. Do not let either set the first year’s architecture budget.

If playtesters solve the leaf without walking the cloister, do not add content. Cut documents until the walk is the only way to know what the witness could see. That result tells you whether the thesis is true.
