# Story Council, Round Three: Should the Player Be Adso?

**Seat:** Literary Adaptation & Character Architect (01-claude-literary)
**Date:** 2026-10-03
**Focus:** whether Adso can carry a long interactive character arc without becoming a puppet of the novel; Adso/William chemistry, voice, adaptation integrity, recurring relationships, and what it means for him to become independent.
**Read:** `README.md`, `docs/PROJECT.md`, `docs/GAME_DESIGN.md`, `docs/PLATFORMS.md`, and the round-two literary report (which summarizes round one's convergence). I did not open any other Round-3 report.
**Spoiler status:** only the owner's approved premises, plus first-pages traits (Adso young, a novice, devoted, impressionable; William curious, dry, empirical). No other novel character is named. Every resident below is **ORIGINAL FICTION** with a placeholder name that someone who has finished the book must check against the cast. ## 0. The short answer

Round two's literary report chose an original clerk and wrote Adso *out* of the telling. That was the more radical breach of the source, not the safer one: inventing small scenes is a normal liberty of adaptation; removing the narrator from his own story is a change in kind. Every reader who meets William in this game will ask "where is Adso?", and every non-reader who meets a nameless young companion will get a sidekick with no literary reason to exist.

The progression hypothesis in the brief (place, time, people, method, independence) is genuinely strong. But I want to be honest about one thing at the start: **none of that progression needs Adso mechanically.** Any apprentice could learn the bells. What Adso adds is *meaning*: a known relationship, a known kind of young man, and a recognizable sense that this apprenticeship matters to literature. So the real question is whether that meaning is worth the constraints he brings. I conclude it is, under one firm structural rule:

> **Facts and major events belong to the source. Attention, order, interpretation, confidence, loyalty in small things, and the written account belong to the player.**

**Verdict in advance: ADAPTED ADSO.** The game is presented as an interactive adaptation, the way a film is an adaptation. It is never presented as hidden chapters of the novel.

## 1. Central Question 1: Does playing Adso make the game better?

### What Adso brings that no original character can

1. **A relationship that already exists.** The William/Adso pair is one of literature's best-known mentor dyads. The player only has to earn its texture, not its premise.
2. **Naivety as identity.** A player who knows nothing about hours, signs or the house is *in character*. Ignorance is not a tutorial excuse.
3. **A thematic match.** The novel concerns reading signs and the gap between seeing and understanding. Agency over *how Adso understands* (§2) is the novel's subject made playable, not a compromise.
4. **Pitch clarity** (§11).
5. **A pre-loaded ending.** An apprenticeship ends in separation or transformation, and with Adso players feel it will matter before the game has earned it. That is a gift and a debt.

### What Adso costs

1. **A canonical spine.** Replace the novel's central situation with an original mystery and the game becomes alternate-universe fan fiction. The spine must be present and protected (§10): spoiler burden, review cost, moments of witnessing rather than acting.
2. **A known voice.** Readers carry an Adso in their heads; every line risks "that isn't him."
3. **A fixed character.** No cruel, cynical, disloyal or worldly Adso. The range is real but bounded.
4. **Inner life the novel owns.** Whatever personal experiences the novel gives him must be decided (played, alluded to, or left to the book) by a reviewer who has finished it. The owner cannot decide this yet.
5. **Original content looks smaller** next to canon unless it is rooted in people the player loves.

### Would players rather be Adso, or an original person beside William?

**Readers:** Adso, overwhelmingly. An original companion with Adso absent reads as omission; with Adso as NPC it reads as demotion. **Non-readers:** mildly Adso, for clarity: "a novice apprenticed to a brilliant, difficult friar" beats "a clerk, sort of an assistant". **Investigation players:** distrust a protagonist whose story is "already written" and need an explicit early promise that their conclusions are their own (§2).

**Judgment:** Adso makes the game *better as a story* and *riskier as a game*. The risk is controllable if agency is placed correctly; it is not if the design pretends the player can steer the novel.

## 2. Central Question 2: Can Adso have player agency?

### The principle: the world's facts are fixed; Adso's understanding is the game

A memoir does not record what Adso noticed moment by moment, in what order, what he scribbled on undescribed afternoons, which lesser people he got to know, or how sure he felt before William spoke. That unwritten space is large, and an adaptation can live there honestly.

### Safe to give the player

| Decision | Why it matters in play |
|---|---|
| **What Adso notices** | Decides which observations exist at all; the core verb. |
| **Investigation order** | Changes which testimony is fresh, which is rehearsed, who is available. |
| **Whom he spends free hours with** | Relationships deepen; residents begin bringing things to Adso. |
| **Which small matters he takes up** | Local outcomes, gratitude, embarrassment, debts (§8). |
| **Interpretation and confidence** | The game evaluates support, not obedience; confidence shapes how others react and whether claims get tested before being repeated. |
| **What goes in the notebook** | The player's version of the stay (§6). |
| **What he tells William, and when** | Shapes what William can reason about; William notices gaps later. |
| **Temporary withholding in small things** | Protecting a friend's minor fault is a believable novice dilemma. Never touches the protected spine. |
| **Approach and exploration** | Asking, watching, waiting, walking and reading are different evidence paths with different social costs. |

### Damaging to the character or source

Changing major events, outcomes or the central solution (a "what if" mode, if ever wanted, is separate and labelled). Solving the central matter earlier or more fully than the source allows. A dialogue "personality" that makes Adso cruel, cynical, mocking of faith, sexually forward or disloyal; the player chooses stance, not soul. Confronting or exposing novel characters in ways that alter their arcs. Affection or approval meters with anyone. Any knowledge Adso could not have; a reader's foreknowledge gives no in-world advantage.

### Should outcomes differ?

Three tiers. **Canonical events** are fixed. **Original matters** differ locally (who is believed, who is embarrassed, whether a friend is reprimanded) and persist in relationships. **The account** is always the player's: two players witness the same events and leave with different notebooks, errors, confidence and trusted people.

Adso is a puppet only if he is walked to canonical scenes and told what they mean. Two rules prevent that:

1. **No canonical scene explains itself through Adso's voice.** The game shows what he could perceive; interpretation stays open until the player builds it, or William argues for one as a character with his own view.
2. **Every canonical scene leaves Adso holding at least one question** the player must resolve or consciously leave open.

## 3. Central Question 3: Adso as progression, with no stats

### The design rule: growth appears as changed affordances and changed behaviour, never as numbers

Adso grows in seven areas. For each one the table gives what changes in play and how the player perceives it without any UI number.

| Area | Early state | Late state | How the player perceives it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Place** | No plan. Every corridor is new. | He knows routes, entrances, which stair connects which floor, what is visible from where, and how long walks take. | The notebook's sketch plan fills in only where he has walked. Later, testimony challenges offer "that route passes the [barred door] at that hour" only if Adso has met that door at that hour. **Late on, people follow Adso**, including William. |
| **Time** | Bells are noise. | Bells carry meaning, he anticipates routines, and he notices when timing is unusual. | **Notebook timestamps sharpen.** Day-one entries read "in the afternoon, after a bell". Later ones read "between Sext and the meal". Later still: "before Vespers; the cantor had not yet gone in". The old entries stay as written. |
| **People** | "A tall monk with inky fingers." | Names, duties, habits, who is usually where, and when someone is acting unusually. | **Entries name people only once Adso has learned their names**, and early descriptions remain as traces. Residents' greetings change from formal to familiar to expectant. |
| **Vocabulary and context** | Theological and political terms pass him by. | He recognizes positions, terms and which words are dangerous. | Overheard speech that was once summarized as "they argued about [the question]" later records which words were used and by whom. The *granularity of hearing* improves. |
| **Observation discipline** | He collapses sight, hearsay and guess into one sentence. | He keeps them apart under pressure. | The notebook's categories emerge through conversation (§6), and the player's own writing becomes cleaner. |
| **Confidence and independence** | He waits for William. | He acts, tests and reports, and sometimes disagrees. | **Who initiates.** First William asks. Then residents ask William for Adso. Then residents come to Adso directly. Finally Adso raises a problem William had not seen. |
| **Access** | He is a guest's boy, tolerated at the edges. | He is trusted by particular people for particular things. | Residents grant small permissions personally: "Stand here during the office; you'll hear better." "Go through the yard; tell the carter I sent you." Access comes from relationships, not keys. |

### William's changing treatment is the main progression meter

This is the most important design idea in this report, and it costs almost nothing to build: **the walking order.**

- **Early:** when they walk together, William leads and Adso follows. William sets the pace, stops to look, and Adso catches up.
- **Middle:** they walk *beside* each other. At a fork William asks, "Which way is shorter at this hour?"
- **Late:** William follows Adso through the house. He doesn't comment on it. The player notices.

The same follow-behaviour system, re-parameterized over days, turns the relationship into blocking. No text box is needed.

Other visible signals:

- **Question length.** Early on, William asks long, guiding questions. Late, he asks "Well?"
- **Who speaks first.** Early on, William opens every report. Late, he waits.
- **Verification.** Early on, he re-checks what Adso tells him. By the midpoint he re-checks some of it. Late, he acts on Adso's word, and says once that he did so.
- **The materials of trust.** Early Adso writes on a wax tablet that is easily smoothed, provisional and never permanent. At an unannounced moment, William gives him a few leaves of parchment: *"For the things you would still say tomorrow."* The physical notebook changes. (**HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION**: wax-tablet use by a novice in this setting needs a cited exemplar before it is adopted.)

### What the player should feel

| Moment | Feeling | What causes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Hour 1** | Wonder, smallness, the pleasure of being looked after. "I don't know where anything is, and that's fine because he does." | Arrival, William leading, bells as mystery. |
| **Hour 3** | The first small competence. "I know this part of the house. I knew where to stand." | Walking a route without thinking, recognizing a face, one correct anticipation of an office. |
| **Midpoint** | Being expected. "People look for me. William asked *me* which way." | Residents approaching, walking side by side, the first disagreement with William that he concedes. |
| **Late campaign** | Responsibility, some loneliness, and quiet pride. "He isn't here, and I'm the one who knows." | William occupied in delegation matters, Adso alone in the house's lower world, and decisions about what to tell. |
| **Ending** | Bittersweet clarity. "I see differently now, and I know what I don't know." | The notebook read back. The walking order remembered. The account owned. |

## 4. Central Question 4: William and Adso

### The relationship's rules

1. **A person, not an interface.** William reads, mutters, is cold, dislikes pomp, enjoys small practical successes, goes quiet. Finding him at his own work *is* content.
2. **Walking conversations are the main medium.** Talk happens on the way; the destination is often incidental.
3. **He asks questions; he never assigns locations.** "What do you make of the porter?", never "Go to the gate."
4. **He is wrong for structural reasons.** He dines higher and is absent from the lower house's hours. About once per in-game day he holds a local assumption Adso's lived experience can correct.
5. **He admits uncertainty, increasingly.** Early as a teaching pose, late for real.
6. **He can lose arguments.** On large matters his breadth and the spine usually win; on local matters Adso, who *was there*, can be right against him.
7. **Humour becomes shared.** Early, his dry jokes at Adso's expense; by midpoint Adso returns them through chosen stances; late, they laugh together at something nobody else would find funny.
8. **One mission-free scene per day**: a meal, a cold walk, questions about home and travels. These carry the relationship.
9. **He never restates the player's discovery more cleverly.** He may ask how, test it or argue.
10. **Later separation is the relationship's climax, not its absence.** Brief reunions while William is in closed rooms become the most charged scenes.

### What Adso eventually becomes capable of

Keeping sight, testimony and thought separate *while being pressed*; moving through the house at any hour knowing what is normal; being trusted by people William never really meets; saying "I don't know, and here is exactly why"; disagreeing with William aloud and standing by it; making a loyalty decision and living with its cost.

### What makes that visible in William

He walks behind Adso (§3). He hands over parchment. He asks Adso's view before giving his own. Once, in Adso's hearing, he repeats something Adso taught him about the house, with attribution. Late, he sends Adso somewhere on his behalf without explaining, because explaining is no longer necessary. In a quiet scene he lets Adso see that he is tired and unsure; a mentor who shows weakness has stopped being only a teacher.

## 5. Central Question 5: Should older Adso frame the game?

- **No framing:** maximum immediacy, but loses memory's atmosphere and any "afterwards". Acceptable, thin.
- **Sparse chapter openings/closings:** an older man knows how everything ended, and every line leaks it, worst at day openings. **Reject.**
- **Reflection after major investigations:** it lands after the player's conclusion but tends to certify or correct it ("the real answer was…"). **Reject** for interpretation.
- **Notebook becomes the memoir:** turns the player's work into the literary artifact; risks implying the game's notebook *is* the canonical memoir. **Adopt, with one rule.**

**Recommendation: no older voice during play; one closing page.** During play the notebook is young Adso's working record. Day headings may echo the novel's marking of days and hours, but without summary lines, because summaries foretell. At the end, the player's own notebook (entries, corrections, the account they stood behind) is shown bound, framed by one short passage in an older voice about memory, the teacher and time, never about the case. ***The older voice may comment on the young man, never on the evidence.*** The game never presents these pages as the source of Eco's memoir and uses no "found manuscript" conceit that would make inventions look like lost canon.

## 6. Central Question 6: Adso's notebook

### The notebook should start undisciplined, and the player should see that

Day-one entries, built from the player's selections, are naive by construction: blurry attribution, opinion mixed with sight, descriptions instead of names:

> *The porter says the brothers always bar the yard gate at dusk. The house is very orderly.*

That fuses a testimony, a generalization and a judgment. The game does not mark it wrong; it records what Adso thought worth writing.

### Categories are taught through conversation, not unlocked as buttons

When Adso reports, William asks *how he knows*, and the player's answer ("I saw it" / "He told me" / "I suppose it") splits the entry. After a few such exchanges the notebook asks the same question at the moment of writing, because *Adso* now asks it of himself. It appears as a margin habit, a small mark he begins to make, never a modal choice.

The five classes emerge over time:

- **OBSERVATION**, "I saw…": present from the start, but early on it is often contaminated.
- **TESTIMONY**, "Brother X told me…": separated after the first William challenge.
- **DOCUMENT**, "The register says…": arrives when Adso first handles a written record with William present, and William asks who wrote it and when.
- **INFERENCE**, "I think…": separated after the first misunderstanding (§7).
- **UNCERTAINTY**, "I cannot establish…": last and most important, arriving mid-campaign after Adso is first confidently wrong in front of someone who matters. An Adso who can write it has grown up a little.

### Revision preserves the trace

The player can return to any earlier entry and annotate it, but never delete it. Annotations appear in a visibly later hand:

> *The porter says the brothers always bar the yard gate at dusk.*
> ↳ *(later) He means on ordinary days. It was not barred the evening the carts came; I passed through it myself.*

The notebook becomes a visible history of Adso's mind; re-reading day one late in the game should be slightly embarrassing and quite moving.

### The notebook is not the main screen

Most reasoning happens in the world and in conversation: walking a route, standing at a window, asking a sharper question, telling William. The notebook is where Adso *keeps* understanding, not where he *does* it. Budget: under 15% of play time with it open.

## 7. Central Question 7: The first day as Adso, 90 minutes

Residents are **ORIGINAL FICTION**. The arrival uses the approved premise only and deliberately does **not** reproduce the novel's arrival chapter; a reviewer should confirm it neither copies nor contradicts it.

**0–12 min. The climb.** Cold, wind, a mule's breath, bells from above before anything is visible. William talks while walking (weather, road, a travel story) and once asks, "What do you hear?" No right answer; the player chooses what to attend to. The walls appear, and the scale makes the player feel small.

**12–25 min. Reception and the side door.** William is received by the house's authorities while Adso stands aside holding the animals, which establishes his station without a word. **Lapo**, a lay stable-hand, takes the mules and asks whether Adso's master is "the one who looks at everything", since rumour has arrived first. *Recurring person 1.*

**25–40 min. The ordinary task.** **Brother Ilario**, the guest-house brother (anxious, hospitable, fussy), gives Adso the lodging and rules too fast to remember. Adso settles William's things: water, books, bedding. *Optional exploration opens:* guest range, yard, cemetery edge, church exterior, with gentle turning-back from places guests don't go.

**40–55 min. The canonical-hour transition.** A bell Adso cannot interpret. **Ottone**, a quick, cheeky novice his own age, pulls him into the church to where guests stand. *Recurring person 2.* The guests' place becomes a fixed vantage the player will later know exactly. Afterward Ottone whispers a joke about the cantor's eyebrows: **the first human moment unrelated to any investigation.**

**55–70 min. The misunderstanding.** In the lodging, a second bedroll is turned down and a candle burnt halfway. Suspicion: someone has been sleeping here. The player records it as they like and reports when William returns.

William: *"What did you see?"*
A bedroll, made up. A candle, half burnt.
*"And what did you not see?"*

**The small intellectual challenge:** before supper, establish whether anyone slept here. Three paths: Brother Ilario issues candles, Ottone knows how guest rooms are prepared, Lapo saw the letter announcing the guests. The answer is ordinary: half-candles are a thrift for guests, and the letter said "with his company", so the bedroll was laid generously. Nobody slept there.

**The reframing moment.** William, quite pleased:

*"You weren't wrong to notice. You were wrong to finish the sentence."*

That line is the game's method. The earlier entry remains, ready to be annotated.

**70–85 min. A human moment and a choice.** Lapo shyly asks Adso to read him a letter from home: a sister, a harvest, a debt. The player chooses how gently to read one hard line. *A structural seed:* Adso is literate, young and not of the house, so its unlettered people will come to him, which is the origin of many original matters (§8).

**85–90 min. "What did you see that I could not?"** At night the player shares two or three notebook items. William offers one of his own and gets a local fact wrong: he assumes a monk rang the supper bell, but Adso saw Ottone ringing it. Corrected, William is delighted.

**The hook.** Three threads pull forward:

- **The central pressure:** William, at the window, says the abbot spoke to him of [the matter troubling the house], and that he would like Adso's eyes "where they will not notice a boy".
- **An obligation:** Lapo's reply must be written tomorrow, and Adso promised.
- **A friendship:** Ottone said he'd show Adso something at dawn, "before the brothers are up".

No crime, but suspicion, competence, a friend and a promise. Every required element is present, including two human moments unrelated to investigation.

## 8. Central Question 8: Adso and original missions

### Yes, if they come from Adso's position rather than the plot's shadow

Test: **would this plausibly reach a literate, young, outsider novice who belongs to no faction in the house?** Passing categories: reading and writing for the unlettered (letters, tallies, receipts people cannot check); novice-world troubles (a lost object before a reprimand, an unfair blame); hospitality friction; communication failures (a message through three mouths, a sign misread at a silent meal); timing discrepancies ("which bell?"); practical problems (a borrowed ladder, an unaccounted lamp); small manuscript questions.

**How many?** The limit is a **weight rule**, not a count: ***no original matter may be weightier than the smallest canonical event the player meets that day.*** In practice, one or two active matters per day and about **8–12 per campaign**. No original deaths, conspiracies or secret places.

- **Mostly optional.** Two or three early ones sit on the main path because they teach method (the half candle); the rest are offered by residents and can be missed.
- **William's knowledge** is the player's choice. Told, he is useful and sometimes wrong (he doesn't know these people). Untold, he may find out later and ask why, as a conversation about judgment rather than a reproach.
- **Residents approach Adso directly, increasingly.** This is the main signal of independence.
- **Each matter changes how at least one person greets Adso**, or it is cut.
- **Original people carry original matters.** Novel characters appear only in public functions (presiding, scolding, passing), never as hidden subjects, culprits or holders of invented secrets.

Never: a novel event with names filed off, a "parallel" of a canonical incident, or foreshadowing of the spine. If it feels like "the book's thing, smaller", discard it. A reviewer who has finished the book applies this test.

## 9. Central Question 9: The papal delegation with Adso

Before the delegation, William mediates the larger world for Adso. Afterwards he *disappears into it*: sessions, tables of rank, rooms Adso cannot enter. The routine bends around strangers, and Adso, after days of living in it, **knows the house better than the visitors and, in its lower half, better than William.**

| William knows | Adso knows |
|---|---|
| What was argued in session | What changed in the house because of it |
| Who outranks whom | Who is actually doing the work |
| What the visitors say they want | What their servants asked for at the kitchen door |
| The politics of blame | The ordinary routine that was disturbed, and why |

### Situations needing both halves (original, spoiler-safe)

1. **The Lost Servant.** Adso guides a visitor's lost servant and hears what he was told to find out. William recognizes from the *subject* of those questions what the visitors are really worried about.
2. **The Late Arrival.** A session starts late and the visitors take it as a slight. Adso knows their own horses blocked the yard route; William knows how to use that ordinary truth to defuse an insult without humiliating anyone.
3. **"The Guests Are Kept Apart."** A visitor asserts the lodgings are private. Adso has slept in that range and knows a shared stair carries voices at night. William needs that before he speaks freely.
4. **The Misplaced Provisions.** Blame cascades toward a lay servant Adso knows. Adso can establish the routine; William judges whether saying so publicly protects the servant or exposes the house; Adso chooses what to give him.
5. **Two Copies.** A draft and a fair copy that Adso carried differ by a few words. Adso knows when each left his keeping; William knows which words matter. Kept small and original; it must not touch [the central matter].

The delegation is where **the walking order fully inverts** and where Adso's loneliness becomes real. Brief reunions (a stair at a late hour, a few minutes of exchanged knowledge) should be written as the emotional peak of the late game. For the first time William *needs* Adso, and both know it.

## 10. Central Question 10: Adaptation philosophy

### Three content zones

| Zone | Contents | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **FIXED** | Adso's identity and essential character (young, devout, loyal, curious, impressionable); William's identity and essential character; their relationship; the broad setting, arrival and length of stay; the historical and intellectual circumstances; the delegation's existence and importance. | Never altered. Adapted only in presentation. |
| **ADAPTABLE** | Daily-life scenes, exploration order, original minor matters, original residents, conversations in unwritten hours, notebook content, interpretation, confidence, small local outcomes, the texture of the relationship. | The player and writers are free within character. Every item is tagged **ORIGINAL_GAME_FICTION** internally. |
| **PROTECTED** | Major novel events, established character outcomes, revelations, the central solution, Adso's canonical inner and personal experiences, the novel's other named characters' private lives. | Experienced, not altered. Presentation is decided by a reviewer who has finished the book, separately from writers of original content. The owner reviews for forward-spoiler safety only once ready. |

### The adaptation policy, in seven sentences

1. This game is an **interactive adaptation**, as a film or stage version is. It selects, compresses and invents, and says so.
2. Nothing invented is presented, in game or in marketing, as a lost, missing or "untold" chapter of the novel.
3. The player can never change a protected event. The player always owns their own understanding of it.
4. Original matters are sized below the canonical events of their day and centred on original people.
5. Novel characters keep their public functions in original matters and never acquire invented secrets.
6. No novel prose is reproduced. William and Adso speak in freshly written dialogue that is faithful in spirit and not imitative in sentence.
7. Every line spoken by Adso or William, and every protected-zone scene, passes two reviews: **canon review** (does it contradict or copy the book?) and **forward-spoiler review** (does it leak what comes later?).

The existing taxonomy in `GAME_DESIGN.md` (`NOVEL_CANON`, `TEXTUAL_INFERENCE`, `HISTORICAL_RECONSTRUCTION`, `ORIGINAL_GAME_FICTION`) already supports this. The zones above are a policy layer over it. I note that `GAME_DESIGN.md` currently says the initial role "avoids automatically turning the player into William or Adso". This verdict would reverse that line. Updating it is an owner decision; I have not changed it.

## 11. Central Question 11: Commercial and audience clarity

| Pitch | Clarity | Appeal | Expectation | Disappointment risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **"Play as Adso and learn to investigate beside William."** | Highest: one name, one relationship, one verb. | Readers strongly (with scrutiny); non-readers clearly; historical players well; investigators only if agency is promised explicitly. | "The novel, playable." | **High:** readers expect the plot; investigators expect to solve the central matter themselves. Needs a precise adaptation subtitle. |
| **"Play an original assistant travelling with William."** | Medium; "original assistant" sounds like a compromise. | Readers weakly ("why not Adso?"); investigators most, because agency is implied. | "Side content in the novel's world." | Readers feel shortchanged; non-readers don't know why William matters. |
| **"Play a monastery clerk who meets William."** | Low as a lead pitch; good as a scenario-pack premise. | Historical players most; readers least, as William becomes a guest star. | "A historical investigation in a famous abbey." | Low for investigators, high for readers who came for William. |

Framing to validate (not final copy): *"An interactive adaptation of a stay at the abbey. Play as Adso: learn the house, its hours and its people beside William, and decide for yourself what you have seen."* The last clause defuses the agency objection without promising plot changes.

Plainly: a named narrator as marketed protagonist is a much stronger use of the source than a reconstructed abbey. Rights are outside this seat, but this verdict's clarity depends on them and must be settled before any public pitch.

## 12. Central Question 12: Production consequences

**Harder with Adso:**

- **Writing and review.** Every Adso/William line needs voice discipline and two-pass review (§10): roughly **1.5–2× review cost per line** for those two characters. Original residents are unaffected.
- **Continuity.** William's knowledge state must track what Adso told him and when (told / not told / told late), on top of the planned claim/observation primitives. A William who "just knows" breaks the relationship.
- **Character design and animation.** In first person Adso is hands, sleeves and others' reactions, but William becomes a hero asset: face, idle life, gesture during walking talk, and lead/match/follow pacing. That pacing system is the single most important animation investment.
- **Interaction.** Interruptible, resumable, route-aware walking dialogue exceeds the current proof's interaction model.
- **Review roles.** A **canon reviewer** who has finished the book owns the protected zone; a **forward-spoiler reviewer** (the owner now) checks leakage. Protected-zone content cannot be scheduled until the canon reviewer exists.
- **Localization.** Each market's established name forms and terminology from published translations, with no translated prose reproduced; per-language terminology lists.
- **Historical review.** Novice-specific claims (tablets, where a guest's boy may stand or enter) each need a cited exemplar.
- **Scenario authoring.** One more gate per matter: the weight rule and the "not the book's thing, smaller" test.

**Easier with Adso:** no character creation, no name/gender/station decisions, no justification of presence (round two had to reason all of these through). Naivety is free onboarding. Residents' behaviour toward a guest's novice is legible. The ending's stakes are pre-loaded.

**Net:** one added reviewer role, a William-knowledge model and a heavier William animation budget, against less identity design and onboarding writing. Narrative QA grows substantially but not enormously, *if* the protected zone stays small and is handled as discrete scenes.

### The judgment the totals don't make

- **Model A (Strict)** fails as a game: an illustrated audiobook with walking. A predetermined boy is less moving than one whose understanding you built.
- **Model D (Abbey assistant)** ties B on points and wins on freedom and safety. But its progression score is low *for a reason that matters*: someone who belongs to the house already knows the place, the hours and the people. The single most beautiful progression this game can offer is learning a strange house until it becomes yours, and D gives most of it away at the start. D's William is a visitor, not a teacher, so the apprenticeship collapses. **D is an excellent premise for a later scenario pack**, after the main telling, where its divided loyalty and local knowledge are fresh.
- **Model C (Original companion)** is the safe production choice. But to exist, it must either delete Adso or demote him. Both options cost more literary credibility than B's invented small scenes. Its emotional ceiling is lower because players have to *learn* to care about a relationship that, with Adso, they already believe in.
- **Model B (Adapted)** has the worst spoiler and review profile of the viable options. It wins because emotional strength, relationship, progression and clarity are what make someone play for ten hours, and B is strongest on exactly those four. Its weaknesses are *process* problems (review roles, protected-zone discipline) rather than *design* problems. Process problems can be staffed. Design problems can't.

## 14. Why playing Adso may be a mistake

1. **The protected spine makes the player a spectator at the decisive moments.** Understanding-as-agency may feel thin exactly when stakes peak.
2. **The owner cannot design or approve the spine yet.** The project's climax depends on finishing a book and on a reviewer who doesn't exist yet.
3. **Readers will police the voice** against an internal standard writers cannot see.
4. **Readers know what happens.** For them, the spine has no suspense; the unwritten layer must carry everything.
5. **Original content will be read as filler** beside "the real story", and rushed.
6. **Interpretive agency can feel like a consolation prize** to players who want to *solve*.
7. **Adso's character narrows the player's range:** no irony, cynicism, transgression or worldliness.
8. **The progression mechanics don't need him.** If his meaning doesn't land in playtest, he is pure constraint.
9. **Review cost compounds** with content and slows iteration when iteration matters most.
10. **Divergence will be called wrong, not adapted.** A film survives that; a long game with dozens of original scenes accumulates it.
11. **It narrows the long-term world.** Adso cannot return for a second stay without inventing a sequel to a closed literary life.
12. **Rights exposure.** If permission for a named-narrator protagonist is unavailable or restrictive, the verdict fails.

## 15. Why not playing Adso may be a mistake

1. **William without Adso is a visible amputation.** Every reader and every review notices.
2. **An original companion is Adso with the serial numbers filed off:** same seat, same function, same naivety, plus effort spent pretending otherwise.
3. **Demoting Adso to NPC is worse than removing him.** He must track canon or contradict it, and the player is always second in William's affection.
4. **The bond starts from zero** and costs hours of writing to reach where Adso begins.
5. **The pitch becomes vague.** "An original assistant" communicates compromise.
6. **The thematic fit is lost.** The novel's concern with seeing is personified in its young witness.
7. **The ending is weaker.** Leaving a teacher in an invented role cannot carry what literature has already made meaningful.
8. **Original identity brings its own cost:** name, gender, station, plausibility, backstory, all work Adso doesn't need.

## 16. Three Adso prototypes

Build each as an **A/B pair**: identical content, with the protagonist presented once as Adso and once as an original companion (different name, two-line introduction, William unchanged). That is the only honest way to learn whether *Adso* matters rather than whether the *content* is good. Test readers and non-readers separately, at least three of each.

### Prototype 1: "Behind, Beside, Ahead" (mentorship over time)

- **Situation:** the same lodging-to-church walk on three compressed days, with house life between. William leads; then walks beside and asks which way; then follows, as Adso leads him to something only Adso has seen. Each walk has one conversation and one local fact William gets wrong.
- **Systems:** lead/match/follow pacing for one NPC, interruptible walking dialogue, one notebook entry per walk with annotation, a told/not-told flag.
- **Why Adso:** does the known relationship amplify the inversion?
- **Validates:** players mention unprompted that William followed them, describe trust or pride, and the Adso variant beats the original on "I cared what William thought of me". Readers accept the voice.
- **Abandon signal:** in both variants William reads as a guide or hint source and no change is noticed, or the Adso variant shows no attachment advantage.

### Prototype 2: "The Bell That Rang Late" (Adso notices what William cannot)

- **Situation:** two compressed days of routine in one small area (a bell, a person crossing, a barred door). On the third, the bell rings late and the person crosses early, for an ordinary original reason. William, never there at that hour, notices nothing. Does the player notice unprompted, and do they tell William, investigate quietly or ask a resident?
- **Systems:** three persistent actors on fixed routes (the slice's planned scope), a bell schedule with one variant, notebook timestamp sharpening, one askable resident.
- **Why Adso:** does "I lived here and he didn't" land as Adso's growth rather than generic observation play?
- **Validates:** at least two-thirds notice unprompted; the sharpened timestamps are named as growth ("I'm writing better notes now").
- **Abandon signal:** players need prompts, or notice equally in the original variant and report no greater meaning with Adso.

### Prototype 3: "While He Is Inside" (independence, loyalty, combined conclusion)

- **Situation:** William is in a closed session. Ottone brings Adso a problem directly: misplaced provisions, with blame heading for a lay servant Adso likes. Ottone was partly at fault and asks Adso to keep that quiet. Adso works it out alone, then meets William on the stair. William has the political half (who is offended, what blame would cost); Adso has the routine half. The full account needs both; withholding gives a weaker but defensible outcome with a different relational cost.
- **Systems:** a resident-initiated matter, a William-unavailable state, the told/withheld model, a short reunion exchange, two local outcomes with persistent greeting changes.
- **Why Adso:** is the loyalty conflict stronger when the master is *Adso's* William, and does Adso's bounded character restrict the choice?
- **Validates:** players hesitate at the choice, call the stair scene the high point, and readers judge both choices "something Adso could do".
- **Abandon signal:** complaints that choices felt restricted "because it's Adso", readers flagging either choice as out of character, or William treated as an answer-checker in both variants.

## VERDICT

**ADAPTED ADSO.**

## WHY

The apprenticeship of a young outsider who learns to see a strange house beside a brilliant, fallible teacher is Adso's story. Building it with an original protagonist removes the narrator from his own seat, which is a more radical change than adding small scenes. Playing Adso gives the game its strongest emotion, clearest pitch and most natural onboarding. His costs are a protected canonical spine, voice scrutiny and review burden. Those costs are controllable if agency lives in attention, order, interpretation, confidence, small loyalties and the written account, and never in changing what the novel fixed. The progression mechanics don't require Adso. The meaning does.

## PLAYER FANTASY

You are a young novice who arrives at a vast, cold, orderly house that is completely strange to you, beside a teacher you admire and do not yet understand. Day by day the bells start to mean something. The corridors become routes, the strangers become people with habits, and your own careless notes become careful ones. People of the house begin to come to you. By the time the powerful arrive and your teacher is taken into rooms you cannot enter, you are the one who knows how the house actually lives, and he needs what only you saw.

## PROGRESSION

Place (sketch plan fills only where walked; later Adso leads others, William included). Time (notebook timestamps sharpen from "after a bell" to exact offices; deviations become noticeable). People (descriptions become names; residents bring matters directly). Method (the five note classes separate through William's questions, not unlocks). Material (wax, then William's parchment). Initiative (William → residents via William → residents directly → Adso before William). No numbers.

## WILLIAM RELATIONSHIP

**Beginning:** William leads every walk, asks long guiding questions, re-checks everything, teases warmly. **Middle:** side by side; he asks which way, concedes local corrections, delegates half-formed questions, hands over parchment. **End:** absorbed by the delegation, he follows Adso, asks his view first, acts on his word and admits real uncertainty; late reunions on stairs and in corridors are the emotional peak.

## FIRST 90 MINUTES

Climb with bells before walls → William received at the front while Adso holds the mules (Lapo, the stable-hand) → settling William's things, first optional exploration (Brother Ilario) → first office, Ottone pulls him to the guests' place and whispers a joke → the second bedroll and half candle: "someone has slept here" → a three-path check finds the ordinary answer; William: *"You weren't wrong to notice. You were wrong to finish the sentence."* → Lapo asks Adso to read a letter from home → night: Adso corrects William about who rang the bell. Hooks: [the matter the abbot raised], a promised reply, Ottone's dawn secret.

## PLAYER AGENCY RULE

**Can change:** attention, order, companions, which small matters, interpretation, confidence, notebook and revisions, what William is told and when, small temporary withholding, local outcomes of original matters, relationships with original residents. **Cannot change:** major events, outcomes, revelations, the central solution, Adso's or William's essential character, novel characters' arcs, anything Adso could not know. Facts are fixed; understanding is the player's.

## ADAPTATION RULE

An openly declared interactive adaptation, never "lost chapters". FIXED / ADAPTABLE / PROTECTED zones as in §10. No novel prose. Every Adso/William line and protected scene passes canon review (someone who has finished the book) and forward-spoiler review.

## ORIGINAL-MISSION POLICY

Matters that would plausibly reach a literate, young outsider novice. Never weightier than the smallest canonical event that day; one or two per day, about 8–12 total, mostly optional, increasingly brought by residents. Each changes how someone greets Adso. Original people carry them; novel characters only in public functions. "The book's thing, smaller" is discarded.

## PAPAL-DELEGATION FUNCTION

It inverts the knowledge relationship: William disappears into rooms of rank, the routine bends, and Adso knows the house's lived half better than the visitors and better than William. Situations require both halves (§9). The walking order completes its inversion.

## CAMPAIGN LENGTH

**About 12 hours player-facing (range 10–15),** across the several days of the stay plus a departure. Early days run longer (orientation, ordinary life) and later days run shorter and denser. No in-game day should exceed about two and a half hours of play.

## THREE PROTOTYPES

1. **"Behind, Beside, Ahead"**: the same walk three times as William goes from leading to following; tests the mentorship inversion.
2. **"The Bell That Rang Late"**: a deviation only someone who lived the hours would notice; tests lived familiarity.
3. **"While He Is Inside"**: William unavailable, a friend's request, a stair reunion combining political and routine knowledge; tests independence and loyalty within Adso's bounded character.

Each built as an A/B pair against an original companion with identical content.

## BIGGEST RISK

**The protected canonical spine turns the player into a spectator at the moments that matter most.** Playing Adso brings the novel's central events into the game. They cannot be changed, the owner cannot yet design or review them, and if the climax is canonical, "you own your understanding" may feel like a consolation prize precisely when players most want to act.

## ABANDON ADSO IF...

- In A/B playtests, the Adso variant shows **no clear advantage** over the original-companion variant on attachment to William, or on wanting to continue, across two of the three prototypes. Constraint without added meaning is pure cost.
- **More than one-third of readers** flag Adso's voice or choices as "not him" in any prototype after one revision pass.
- **More than one-third of players** describe the choices as restricted "because it's Adso", or call interpretation-based agency "not real choice".
- Players treat original matters as filler and ask "when does the real story start?" before the end of the first-day prototype.
- A canon reviewer who has finished the book cannot be found. Or the protected-zone review of a 30-minute prototype script takes longer than writing it.
- Rights and permission for a named Adso protagonist are unavailable or restrict the adaptation policy above.

If Adso is abandoned, the notebook, walking-order, residents and progression design transfer unchanged to Model C. Nothing built for these prototypes is wasted.

## GAME THESIS

An apprenticeship is the story of learning to see. Played as Adso, this game turns the novel's concern with signs and understanding into the player's own work. The abbey's facts and the novel's great events stand fixed, as they do in memory. What changes is the person looking at them. You arrive unable to tell a bell from a summons or a rumour from a sight, and you leave knowing a house, its people and its hours well enough that the teacher who once led you now follows you through its corridors. The game's truth is fixed. Your understanding of it is yours.

## PLAYER PROMISE

"When you play this game, you will become Adso, arriving as a stranger beside William. You will learn a living abbey's places, hours and people until they become your own knowledge rather than his. You will keep what you saw apart from what you were told, and decide for yourself what you have understood. And you will feel the moment your teacher begins to walk behind you."
